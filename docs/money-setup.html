Wheels for Chris · Money setup

Getting paid, straight to Chris

How to take raffle money so it lands in Chris's own bank account, stays clean if anyone asks, and does not need anybody's credit card details.

The one rule

The money goes to Chris. Nowhere else, not even for a minute.

Do not run it through these

  • An IEL account, or any business account belonging to a provider
  • A support worker's own bank account, even to "hold it for him"
  • A shared account nobody can point at afterwards

Chris is an NDIS participant and some of the people helping are his support workers. That is fine, and it is what mates do. What turns it into a problem is money from the public sitting in a worker's or a provider's account with a participant's name on it. One straight line from the buyer to Chris and there is nothing to explain.

The clean version, in one sentence

Buyers pay into an account in Chris's name, one named person keeps the ticket list, and anyone who asks can see both.

Step one

Set up the account, 20 minutes at the bank

Chris almost certainly does not want raffle money mixed into his everyday spending. Nearly every Australian bank lets you open a second savings account under the same login, free, in a few minutes on the app.

  1. Open a second savings account in Chris's nameSame bank, same login. Call it something obvious like "Wheels for Chris". It is his account, in his name, and he controls it.
  2. Set up a PayID on that accountUse his mobile number or an email address. PayID means people can pay by typing one thing instead of a BSB and an account number, and it works from any Australian bank app instantly.
  3. Write the details down onceAccount name, BSB, account number, PayID. These go on the website and on the printed tickets.
  4. Turn on notificationsSo Chris and whoever is keeping the ticket list both see money the second it lands.

Is it safe to publish a BSB and account number? Yes. Account details only let people put money in, not take it out. Every charity and footy club in the country prints them. Do not publish anything else: no card numbers, no photo of a card, no ID documents.

Step two

Pick how people pay

Start with the free one. Add card payments later if the raffle takes off.

Bank transfer and PayID

Start here

People pay straight into Chris's account from their own banking app. No signup, no account to open, no approval to wait for. It works today.

The catch
Somebody has to match payments to names by hand and send the ticket numbers back. For a few hundred tickets that is a job for one person and a spreadsheet.

An Australian raffle platform

Worth a look

Sites like RaffleLink and Raffletix are built for exactly this. They take the card payment, issue ticket numbers automatically, email the buyer, and run the draw for you. Money settles into a nominated bank account, which would be Chris's.

Fees
A cut of each ticket plus card fees. Check the current rate on their site.
Setup
An application, and they will ask who is running the raffle and how it sits with Tasmanian minor gaming rules. No permit is needed for this one, so that is a short answer.
Check first
Some platforms only take registered charities or incorporated clubs, not individuals. Ask them straight out before building anything around it. If they say no to an individual, running it under a local club that says yes is the usual fix.

Stripe or Square payment links

Read the rules first

Both let you make a payment link with no website work, and both settle into a nominated bank account. But both list lotteries and raffles as restricted businesses, so an account can be shut down after the money is taken if you have not cleared it with them.

Fees
Roughly 1.7% plus 30c per transaction for Australian cards. Confirm current rates before quoting them to anybody.
Setup
Chris's ID and his bank details. His hands only, nobody else's.
Check first
Ask their support in writing whether a permitted charity raffle is allowed on the account, and keep the answer.

GoFundMe

Not for this

Chris already has one and it worked. But a GoFundMe is a donation, and a raffle sells a chance to win something. Do not run raffle ticket sales through a donation page. If somebody wants to give money without buying a ticket, point them at the GoFundMe and keep the two separate.

Step three

Keep the list, because a raffle is a promise

Once someone pays, they own a numbered chance in a draw. That means somebody has to be able to prove which numbers are theirs. One spreadsheet does it.

ColumnWhat goes in it
Date paidThe date it landed in the account
NameWhatever came through on the transfer
ContactMobile or email, so you can send numbers and ring a winner
AmountWhat they paid
Ticket numbersIssued in order, never reused. 0041 to 0043
Sent?Ticked once their numbers have gone back to them

Two habits that save the whole thing.

  • Issue numbers in strict order and never skip. The last number issued is also your ticket count for the odds you publish on draw day.
  • Reconcile the spreadsheet against the bank account once a week. If the totals do not match, find out why that week, not on draw day.
Step four

Put the details on the website

The site has one CONFIG block at the top. Fill in these lines and everything else updates itself.

LineWhat to put in it
payNameThe account name exactly as the bank shows it
payBsbThe BSB
payAcctThe account number
payIdThe PayID, if you set one up
checkoutUrlLeave empty until a card option is live. While it is empty the buy buttons take people to the bank transfer details instead of a dead link.
raised and ticketsSoldUpdate from the spreadsheet whenever you like. The progress bar and the counter follow.

Keep it clean

The three things that keep this above board

  • The permit question is closed. Checked 21 September 2026: this raffle does not need a minor gaming permit. Keep that answer and its date with the ticket list.
  • Support workers help as mates, not on shift. Nobody should be claiming NDIS-funded support hours for time spent selling raffle tickets. Same job, wrong money, and it is the thing an auditor would pick up.
  • Every dollar traceable to Chris. One account in his name, one spreadsheet, published totals. If anyone ever asks where the money went, the answer is a screenshot.
